UrbanCart Retention Analysis: Key Observations Narrative

This analysis explored customer retention at UrbanCart using descriptive analysis at the univariate, bivariate, and multivariate levels. The goal was not to confirm causal relationships or run formal hypothesis tests, but to identify the patterns in the data that appear most relevant to retention and that should guide deeper testing later.

The customer base is broad and relatively balanced across cities, with no single market dominating the dataset. Customers are spread across 12 cities, and the distribution is fairly even, which suggests that the retention patterns observed are not being driven by only one geography. Demographically, the largest customer group is ages 26-35, followed by 36-45, while gender is close to evenly split between female and male customers. One of the clearest profile-level patterns is device usage: most customers prefer the mobile app over the web platform. This suggests that the mobile app is the dominant engagement channel and should remain central in future retention strategy discussions.

At an overall level, retention in this dataset is strong. Most customers are repeat customers, and cohort-level retention remains consistently high across signup and first-order months. Although 30-day retention shows some month-to-month variation, 60-day and 90-day retention are generally very strong across cohorts. This suggests that once customers come back after their first order, many continue to stay active. At the same time, the fact that 30-day retention is more variable than the later windows suggests that the earliest post-acquisition period is the most important point for retention risk.

The strongest descriptive signal in the analysis comes from the delivery experience variables. Customers with worse delivery experiences tend to show lower retention, especially in the shorter 30-day window. Average delay is particularly informative. Customers with very high average delay, especially those in the 15+ minute bucket, show meaningfully weaker retention than customers whose average delays are moderate or low. Customer satisfaction tells a similar story. As average customer rating increases, retention also tends to rise, with the highest-rated customers consistently showing the strongest return behavior. On-time delivery rate also appears related to retention, although its pattern is less perfectly linear than delay and rating. Taken together, these results strongly suggest that service reliability and the quality of the post-order experience are central to whether customers return.

Promotion behavior shows a more nuanced pattern. Customers do not simply become more retained as promotion usage increases. Instead, the relationship appears mixed and likely depends on customer type and usage context. This is important because it suggests promotions may be supporting engagement differently across groups rather than having one universal effect. Since the available data captures promotion usage but not personalization exposure, the analysis can only speak to discount- or promo-linked behavior, not to whether individualized marketing itself improves retention.

The platform usage breadth variables produce another important signal. Customers who engage with UrbanCart across more vendor categories or more delivery types generally appear more likely to be retained than customers whose usage remains narrow. This pattern is especially visible when comparing single-category users with multi-category users, and single-service users with multi-service users. In practical terms, customers who use UrbanCart for several needs, such as restaurants, grocery, pharmacy, or retail, seem more embedded in the platform than customers who use it for only one purpose. This supports the idea that broader platform adoption may be linked to stronger retention.

Customer spending patterns reveal a more balanced, middle-range effect rather than a simple high-is-better relationship. Retention tends to be strongest for customers with moderate average order value and moderate basket size. Customers in the middle spending bands, especially those with average order values around the midrange and with roughly 5 to 7 items per order, show stronger retention than both very low-spend and very high-spend customers. This suggests that the most stably retained customers may be those using UrbanCart in a regular, habitual way rather than in very small or unusually large purchasing situations.

The multivariate views reinforce several of these themes. When delivery quality dimensions are viewed together, retention is strongest among customers who combine high satisfaction with reasonably strong delivery performance. Similarly, the breadth-of-usage patterns remain visible when examined jointly with service breadth and customer segment, which suggests that the association between broader usage and retention is not just a one-variable artifact. Promotion usage also looks more informative when stratified by customer segment and device, supporting the idea that promotional behavior likely interacts with the way different customer groups use the platform.

Taken together, the descriptive story is that UrbanCart retention appears to be shaped by three broad forces. First, operational reliability matters: poor delivery experiences are associated with weaker retention. Second, broader platform adoption matters: customers who use more categories and services appear more likely to stay active. Third, behavioral intensity matters in a non-linear way: moderate, repeatable usage patterns look healthier than extremes in spending or basket size. Promotions are relevant, but their role appears more conditional and less straightforward than delivery quality or breadth of usage.

Overall, the findings suggest that UrbanCart’s strongest retention opportunities may lie in protecting the early customer experience, reducing delay-related friction, maintaining high delivery satisfaction, and encouraging customers to expand into multiple categories and service types. These insights provide a strong descriptive foundation for the next stage of the project, where formal hypothesis testing can be used to evaluate which of these observed patterns are statistically reliable and most meaningful for business decision-making.
