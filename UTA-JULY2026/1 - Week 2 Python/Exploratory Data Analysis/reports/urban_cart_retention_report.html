UrbanCart Customer Retention Study

What keeps customers coming back after their first order?

This report combines exploratory analysis and formal hypothesis testing to identify the operating signals most closely linked to customer retention at UrbanCart. The emphasis is practical: which experiences appear to build repeat behavior, and which levers look weaker than expected.

Executive takeaway

Retention at UrbanCart is strong overall, but the first-order experience still matters. The clearest signal in both the descriptive analysis and the hypothesis tests is customer satisfaction: higher first-order ratings consistently align with higher return rates. Promotions, by contrast, do not show a statistically reliable retention lift.

Problem Statement

The business question is not whether retention matters, but which parts of the customer experience are shaping it most.

UrbanCart operates in a highly competitive delivery market where retaining existing customers is more efficient than constantly replacing them with new acquisitions. The project goal was to identify the factors most associated with continued activity and repeat ordering.

Three hypotheses were originally proposed around delivery reliability, promotion-driven engagement, and breadth of platform usage. Based on your latest direction, this report formally tests the first two and leaves the third outside the final inferential scope.

Primary KPIs

Customer retention rate Repeat order rate Average order frequency

For the formal tests, retention is defined as whether a customer returns within 30, 60, or 90 days after the first order, using only customers with a full observation window for each metric.

Data Overview

The available data is broad enough for strong retention analysis, especially on operations and promotion usage.

Raw sources

`customers.csv`, `orders.csv`, and `deliveries.csv` form a complete customer-order-delivery chain with no reported missing values in the project review.

Coverage

5,000 customers generated 73,022 orders and 73,022 delivery records across 12 cities and 8 states.

Behavioral shape

The customer base is mobile-first, geographically distributed, and heavily repeat-oriented, which makes it a strong setting for retention analysis.

Descriptive Analysis

The descriptive layer pointed to one dominant storyline: early experience quality appears more important than promotional intensity.

Delivery quality stood out immediately.

Customers with cleaner delivery experiences were more likely to come back. In the 30-day eligible cohort, on-time first orders retained at 82.1% versus 77.5% for delayed orders.

Satisfaction moved almost step-for-step with retention.

First-order ratings rose alongside return rates, from 70.8% for rating 1 to 85.3% for rating 5. That makes customer rating the cleanest descriptive indicator in the project.

Promotions looked mixed rather than powerful.

30-day retention was 81.5% for promo users and 80.6% for non-promo users. The raw difference exists, but it is small.

The early window is the most sensitive one.

Retention rises from 81.0% at 30 days to 97.6% at 90 days, which suggests that customers who return once often continue to stay active.

Hypothesis Testing Results

Formal testing was run on the first two approved hypotheses using first-order cohorts and 30, 60, and 90-day retention windows.

Hypothesis 1: Delivery reliability

Supported in direction. The strongest result came from customer rating, which remained statistically significant after adjustment in both the 30-day and 60-day logistic models.

  • 30-day model: customer rating OR = 1.357, 95% CI [1.252, 1.470], p < 0.0001.
  • 60-day model: customer rating OR = 1.459, 95% CI [1.283, 1.658], p < 0.0001.
  • Delayed first orders had lower raw retention, and delivery-status chi-square tests were significant across all three windows.
  • Delay minutes alone were not a stable adjusted predictor once the broader model controls were added.

Hypothesis 2: Promotion-driven engagement

Not supported. Promotions did not deliver a statistically reliable retention lift in either the bivariate tests or the adjusted first-order logistic models.

  • 30-day model: promo-used OR = 1.062, 95% CI [0.908, 1.241], p = 0.4511.
  • 60-day model: promo-used OR = 1.217, 95% CI [0.935, 1.585], p = 0.1443.
  • Discount depth among promo users also failed to show a reliable positive effect.
  • Personalization remains untestable because the data does not include campaign, recommendation, or CRM exposure fields.

Key Insights

The combined exploratory and inferential view converges on a clear set of operational lessons.

1. The first order is a retention test.

The 30-day window carries the most contrast, which means early customer experience management is likely more valuable than waiting to rescue churn later.

2. Satisfaction is the best leading signal.

Rating performance appears to capture more than simple timeliness. It likely reflects the overall impression of trust, fulfillment quality, and convenience.

3. Promotions are weaker than service quality.

Discounts may help conversion or trial, but this dataset does not show that they create durable customer return behavior on their own.

Recommendations

These recommendations prioritize what the analysis suggests will have the highest retention payoff.

Operational actions

  • Protect the first-order journey with tighter monitoring on delayed deliveries, especially orders at risk of slipping into the 15+ minute bucket.
  • Use customer rating as an operational north-star metric for early retention, not just as a service score.
  • Create a first-order recovery workflow for delayed or low-rated orders, such as service follow-up, apology credits, or support outreach.

Commercial actions

  • Reassess blanket promotion spend, since discounts do not appear to generate strong retention lift by themselves.
  • Shift testing toward service-led retention strategies and measure whether operational improvements outperform discounting.
  • Invest in better personalization data capture so future analysis can test CRM exposure, recommendations, and targeted offers directly.

Limitations

The findings are useful and directionally strong, but they still sit inside a few important data constraints.

No direct personalization data

Campaign exposure, recommendation impressions, push notifications, and loyalty interactions are not available, so the personalization half of Hypothesis 2 cannot be tested.

Very high 90-day return rates

Because almost everyone in the eligible 90-day cohort returned, the adjusted logistic models at that window became unstable and should be interpreted carefully.

Retention is inferred, not stored

The dataset does not contain a native churn label, so retention depends on how the return window is defined and on having enough time to observe it.
